Natural Blend

Natural Indicator — drag onto the strip
9-slot pH test strip

Our indicator solution is a cold-steeped blend of blueberry extract, red cabbage, and turmeric. Blueberries and red cabbage both carry anthocyanins, pigments that shift from deep red through violet and blue as acidity drops, albeit not as noticeable, due to the turmeric extract being way too powerful compared to others. Turmeric contributes curcumin, which stays strong orange in acid but turns a deep red-brown once a solution turns basic. Combined, the three plant extracts stretch the visible color change across the full range of a 9-slot strip — dip it in, and each slot blooms into the shade that matches its condition, without a single synthetic dye.

Blueberry Extract

Blueberry Extract — vanilla in acid, dark red in base
5-slot pH test strip

Blueberry extract acts as a natural pH indicator because it contains anthocyanins, water-soluble flavonoid pigments responsible for many red, purple, and blue colours found in plants. Changes in hydrogen ion concentration alter the chemical structure of anthocyanins, causing visible colour shifts. In acidic solutions, anthocyanins generally appear red or pink, while increasing alkalinity causes purple, blue, and sometimes greenish tones.

Red Cabbage

Red Cabbage — reddish pink in acid, baby blue in base
5-slot pH test strip

Red cabbage is one of the most common natural pH indicators because it contains a high concentration of anthocyanins called cyanidin derivatives. These pigments respond strongly to changes in acidity, producing a wide colour spectrum. Acidic conditions stabilise red forms of the pigment, neutral conditions produce purple colours, and alkaline conditions shift the pigment towards blue, green, and yellow-green shades.

Turmeric

Turmeric — stays deep yellow-brown in acid, deep red-brown in base
3-slot pH test strip

Turmeric contains curcumin, a natural polyphenolic pigment responsible for its bright yellow colour. Unlike anthocyanins, curcumin does not display a large colour range across the whole pH scale. Instead, it acts as a selective alkaline indicator. In acidic and neutral solutions, curcumin remains yellow, while strongly basic solutions cause structural changes that produce orange-red to reddish-brown colours.

Recipes

Generic Natural Indicator Extraction Method

1. Chop or grind the plant material into small pieces to increase surface area.

2. Add the material to warm distilled water and allow pigments to diffuse into the solution. Avoid boiling because excessive heat can degrade some plant pigments.

3. Filter the extract through filter paper or a fine cloth to remove solid particles.

4. Store the indicator solution in a sealed container away from direct sunlight to slow pigment degradation.

Blueberry Indicator

Crush approximately 200 g of blueberries and steep in 1000 mL of warm distilled water for 15–30 minutes. Filter the liquid before use.

Red Cabbage Indicator

Chop approximately 200 g of red cabbage and steep in 1000 mL of hot distilled water. After the liquid becomes dark purple, filter and cool before testing.

Turmeric Indicator

Mix 50 g of turmeric powder with 1000 mL of ethanol. Filter the suspension to obtain a yellow curcumin-containing indicator solution.

Literature Review

Anthocyanins as pH-Responsive Pigments

Anthocyanins are flavonoid pigments whose core structure, the flavylium cation, exists in several interconverting forms depending on the surrounding hydrogen ion concentration. At low pH the flavylium cation dominates and produces a red colour; as pH rises the molecule loses protons and passes through blue quinoidal-base and colourless hemiketal forms before breaking down into pale chalcone structures at strongly alkaline pH. This equilibrium is what allows blueberry and red cabbage extracts to sweep through red, purple, blue, and eventually green or yellow-green tones across a single test strip. Spectroscopic characterisation of these transitions is well established in the food chemistry literature.

Structural Differences Between Sources

Not all anthocyanin sources behave identically. Red cabbage owes most of its colour range to cyanidin derivatives, which are reported to give a particularly wide and reliable spectrum from red through purple to blue-green as alkalinity increases. Blueberries contain a more varied mixture of anthocyanidins, which is consistent with the blend used here, where the blueberry component reinforces the red and pink end of the scale while the cabbage extract carries the shift toward blue. Reviews of anthocyanin chemistry also note that these pigments have documented antioxidant and health-related properties beyond their use as indicators, which is part of why they are popular in food-science teaching demonstrations.

Curcumin as a Selective Alkaline Indicator

Curcumin, the principal pigment in turmeric, behaves differently from anthocyanins. Rather than tracking the full pH range, curcumin is described in the literature as staying essentially unchanged and yellow across acidic and neutral conditions, only undergoing a structural change to a red-brown form once the solution becomes strongly basic. This makes curcumin useful as a high-pH marker rather than a general-purpose indicator, and explains why the turmeric strip in this project only needs three reference slots instead of five or nine.

Why a Blend Extends the Range

Because anthocyanins and curcumin respond to different parts of the pH scale through different chemical mechanisms, combining them broadens the range of visually distinct colours available on one strip. This is consistent with general practice in natural-indicator literature, where blends of pigment sources are used to compensate for the narrower range of any single plant extract. The trade-off observed in this project's own blend is that curcumin's colour is strong enough to dominate the mixture, muting the finer anthocyanin transitions — a practical limitation worth noting alongside the theoretical advantage of blending.
